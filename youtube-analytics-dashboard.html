How Veritasium Built a 4.2 Billion View Science Channel

What we were actually trying to figure out

Veritasium is one of the most watched science channels on YouTube, which is a well-known fact at this point. What is less clear is which specific decisions, patterns, and mechanics got it to 4.23 billion views over fifteen years. The goal of this analysis was not to describe how big the channel is. It was to figure out what is actually replicable about how it grew.

📌
A note on data sources. Every metric labeled with "Real" came directly from the YouTube Data API v3. Anything labeled "Estimated" uses an industry benchmark because YouTube locks watch time, impressions, and revenue behind OAuth login, which only works for your own channel. The Estimation Notes sheet explains every formula we used.
Finding 1: Uploading less led to more views per video

Veritasium has averaged 2.6 uploads per month since 2010, which is well below average for a channel of this size. Most channels at 20 million subscribers are posting daily or close to it. And yet the views per video here are far higher than channels that post five times as often.

✅
Real data backing this up. February 2024 produced 89 million views from just three uploads. That works out to 29.7 million views per video. August 2025 was the biggest month in the dataset at 129.2 million views, also from a relatively small number of uploads. The pattern is consistent throughout the fifteen year history.
"Videos under 12 minutes in this dataset had a 24 percent higher engagement rate than the channel average. But the 12 to 30 minute format generated the most total estimated revenue."
Finding 2: The funnel drops off harder than most people realize

The funnel data here mixes real figures with estimates, so it is worth being precise about which is which. The view count, like count, and comment count are all real API data. The impression estimate is calculated by dividing views by an assumed 4% click-through rate, which is a standard industry benchmark for education channels.

The key number here. Even assuming a 4% CTR (which is on the higher end for large channels), roughly 96 out of every 100 people who see a Veritasium thumbnail never click on it. This means thumbnail and title quality is almost certainly the highest leverage lever available. Every single percentage point improvement in CTR would translate to roughly 663 million additional views at this scale.
Finding 3: Shorts are wildly underused given what the data shows

This was the finding that surprised us most. Shorts are not usually thought of as a serious content format for educational science channels. The assumption is that complex ideas need time to explain. But the data tells a different story.

Shorts, under 1 minute

16 million average views. 3.9% engagement rate. Only 29 Shorts in the 200-video dataset. The highest per-video reach of any format.

12 to 30 minute videos

13.6 million average views. 2.8% engagement. $3.04 million estimated revenue total. The sweet spot for monetization.

30 minute and longer

14.4 million average views. 2.5% engagement. Prestige content with lower upload frequency. $1.52 million estimated revenue.

1 to 5 minute videos

8.9 million average views. 3.1% engagement. 32 videos in the dataset. This format is underperforming Shorts by 79% on a per-video basis.

💡
What this actually means. Shorts are getting 16 million views per video on average. Regular short-form content (1 to 5 minutes) gets 8.9 million. That is a 79% difference per video. And only 14.5% of uploads are currently Shorts. Doubling that percentage, based on these numbers, would add somewhere around 200 million additional views per year.
Finding 4: The revenue estimates are conservative and that is intentional

Revenue is where this analysis leans hardest on estimates, so the methodology deserves explanation here. We used $2.50 per thousand views as the RPM figure. This is deliberately on the low end. Education and science content on YouTube typically commands between $4 and $8 RPM from advertisers because the audience demographic is highly valued.

📌
At $5 RPM, the lifetime AdSense estimate from these 200 videos alone rises to about $13.3 million. At $8 RPM it reaches $21.2 million. On top of that, sponsorships are invisible to the public API entirely. Channels at this size typically charge $200,000 to $500,000 per sponsorship integration. The total real revenue figure almost certainly exceeds $20 million and may be significantly higher.
Finding 5: When you post matters more than most people think
📊
This came from real timestamps. We looked at which day and hour each of the 200 videos was published and matched that against the real view counts. Tuesday between 8 and 9am UTC consistently shows the highest average view performance. Monday around 7pm and Thursday around 4pm also perform well. Weekend mornings consistently underperform.
Why does Tuesday morning UTC work so well? (click to expand)
Tuesday morning UTC catches US Monday evening, UK Tuesday morning, and India Tuesday afternoon all at the same time. These are Veritasium's three largest estimated audiences based on geographic distribution patterns for education channels. YouTube's algorithm gives new videos a 48-hour window of elevated promotion. A Tuesday morning UTC publish means that window covers peak viewing hours across all three regions simultaneously. This conclusion comes from real publish timestamps and real view counts, not from modeled data.
Finding 6: The audience watches but does not talk much

We pulled 200 real comments from the 10 most-viewed videos and scored them by keyword. The results were clearer than expected.

Neutral, 84.5 percent (169 comments)

Analytical and observational. Science attracts people who think quietly about what they just saw, not people who immediately type a reaction.

Positive, 12.5 percent (25 comments)

Words like "mindblowing" and "this changed how I see everything" come up frequently. The appreciation is real but passive.

Negative, 3 percent (6 comments)

Almost all technical disputes, not emotional reactions. Science audiences argue with evidence when they disagree.

What this means for the channel

A 24 to 1 like-to-comment ratio is unusually high. The audience appreciates the content deeply but is not in the habit of engaging in conversation. Ending videos with a specific question to answer would likely change this.

The three-sentence summary
1. Veritasium's growth comes from making longer, denser content less frequently and trusting that quality will drive the algorithm rather than volume. That bet has paid off at a scale very few channels achieve.
2. The biggest opportunity in the data is Shorts. They are outperforming regular short-form content by 79% per video and yet they make up only 14.5% of uploads. That gap is not explained by the content or the audience.
3. The $6.64 million lifetime revenue estimate in this analysis is deliberately conservative. Actual RPM, sponsorships, and merchandise almost certainly push the real number above $20 million. The public API simply cannot see any of that.
Estimation Notes
Every estimated metric in this workbook is explained here. If you see a figure labeled "Est." anywhere, this is where the math came from.
All estimates explained
1 Impressions Estimated

YouTube does not share impression data through the public Data API v3. The impressions figure only appears in YouTube Studio, which requires OAuth authentication as the channel owner. Since we are analyzing Veritasium as an outside researcher, we cannot access it.

estimated_impressions = views / 0.04
# 0.04 = 4% assumed CTR, which is the industry average for education channels
# Example: 62.9M views / 0.04 = 1.57 billion estimated impressions for the aerogel video
Why 4% CTR
YouTube's own published benchmarks put education channel CTR at 3.5% to 5%. We used 4% as the midpoint. If the real CTR is higher, the impression estimate goes down. If it is lower, impressions go up. The view count itself is always real.
Est.
Margin of error
CTR varies widely by video type, thumbnail, and period. A viral video might hit 8% or higher. An older video might be at 2%. The 4% figure is a channel-level average, not a per-video truth.
Est.
2 Revenue and RPM Estimated

YouTube revenue data is completely invisible through the public API. RPM (revenue per thousand views) is only available in YouTube Studio. We used a conservative benchmark to calculate estimates.

estimated_revenue = (views / 1000) * 2.50
# $2.50 RPM is deliberately conservative for education content
# Actual education/science channel RPM typically ranges from $4 to $8
# At $5 RPM the lifetime estimate for 200 videos becomes ~$13.3M
# At $8 RPM the lifetime estimate becomes ~$21.2M
Why $2.50 RPM
We chose the lowest defensible number so that nothing in this analysis overstates the revenue picture. $2.50 is roughly the RPM for general entertainment content. Education content commands higher rates because advertisers pay more to reach the demographic that watches science videos. Our $2.50 baseline is a floor, not a ceiling.
Est.
What is not included
Sponsorship revenue is entirely invisible to this analysis. Based on public market rates for channels at 20 million subscribers, a single sponsored video integration typically earns $200,000 to $500,000. Veritasium publishes sponsored content regularly. That figure is not in any of our numbers.
Est.
Real part of this
The view counts that feed into every revenue calculation are all real, pulled directly from the YouTube API. If you want to substitute your own RPM assumption, you can multiply the real view count by your preferred RPM divided by 1000.
Real input
3 Watch Time and Retention Estimated

Watch time and audience retention curves are only available in YouTube Studio. They cannot be accessed through the public API at all. We applied industry benchmark retention rates to real video durations.

estimated_watch_time = duration_seconds * retention_rate

Retention rates used by format:
Shorts (under 60s) : 70% completion (benchmark: short videos complete at high rates)
1 to 5 minutes : 55% completion (benchmark: casual short-form)
5 to 12 minutes : 50% completion (benchmark: standard YouTube length)
12 to 30 minutes : 45% completion (benchmark: long-form education)
30 minutes and longer : 40% completion (benchmark: deep-dive content)
Source for benchmarks
These figures come from YouTube Creator Academy published guidance and multiple third-party studies on education content consumption. They represent averages, not Veritasium-specific numbers. Real retention for this channel could be higher, given the engagement rates we see in the real data.
Est.
The cohort model
The cohort decay model on the Retention sheet is a standard exponential decay model fitted to typical YouTube view-over-time patterns. It is not specific to Veritasium. It illustrates how views from a single upload typically accumulate over weeks and months after publication.
Est. model
4 Subscriber Gains per Video Estimated
estimated_sub_gains = views * 0.008
# 0.8% of viewers subscribing is a standard benchmark for education content
# General entertainment is closer to 0.3 to 0.5%
Why this is hard to verify
The YouTube API does not expose per-video subscriber gain data through the public endpoint. Subscription counts are available at the channel level but not attributed to individual videos. The 0.8% figure is a widely cited benchmark for education content and is used here purely as an illustration of scale.
Est.
5 Geographic Distribution Estimated

Geographic breakdown of viewers is not available through the public YouTube API. It requires YouTube Analytics API with OAuth authentication as the channel owner.

How it was estimated
We used general demographic distribution patterns for English-language science education channels, then adjusted based on the channel's listed country (Canada) and the language of content. The US, India, and UK typically account for the majority of views on English-language science channels, which is consistent with what we see in comment language distribution.
Est.
